Humanoid robot proof of concept in your company: phases, KPIs and risks

13 July 2026Updated 9 October 2026Abra Robotics
In brief: a humanoid PoC answers one precise question (does this robot do this task, at this pace, with this much human intervention?) before you invest in more units. It works when KPIs, scope and success criteria are written down before the robot is switched on. The timelines given here are indicative and depend on the case.

A humanoid robot PoC is not buying the robot and seeing what happens. It is an experiment with a defined scope, metrics written down in black and white, and a final decision: go ahead, change approach or stop. This guide describes a method a company can follow, the KPIs to set, how to choose the Unitree platform and which safety rules to keep in mind.

What a PoC must demonstrate

A PoC answers a single question, framed so that it can be verified. Examples of well-framed questions:

  • Does the robot pick boxes from a pallet and place them on the conveyor with an acceptable success rate?
  • Does the robot collect teleoperation data that can be used to train a manipulation policy?
  • Does the robot complete a visual inspection round of an area without operator intervention?

Questions like "let's see what it can do" or "let's do a demo" produce videos, not decisions. If the question is not clear, the first step is a process analysis, not a purchase.

The KPIs to write down before you start

A KPI set after the tests always adapts to the result. That is why they must be written in the plan, with a threshold value. The most common:

  • Success rate: tasks completed over tasks attempted, across a minimum number of trials.
  • Cycle time: seconds per task, compared with the current figures.
  • Human interventions: how many times per working hour the operator has to correct, reposition or stop the robot.
  • Real battery life: actual working hours per battery, compared with the manufacturer's figure (about 2 hours for the G1, about 6 hours for the G1-D with mobile base, about 1 hour for the R1).
  • Setup time: how long it takes to get the robot back into operation after a task change or a fall.

Example with hypothetical numbers: a success threshold of 90% over 200 picks, a maximum cycle time of 20 seconds, no more than 2 interventions per hour. If the PoC closes at 80% with 6 interventions per hour, the report must say why (grasping, perception, positioning) and whether there is a plausible fix.

Safety and rules to consider

General information, not legal advice.

Today machines placed on the EU market follow the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC. From 20 January 2027 Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies, repealing the directive from that same date. For industrial robots the technical reference is the ISO 10218 series, revised in 2025. For dynamically stable mobile robots (legged or on balancing wheels) ISO is developing ISO 25785-1, not yet published as a final standard at the time of writing.

In practice, for the PoC:

  • A cordoned-off test area, with controlled access during testing.
  • A biped in an emergency stop can fall, because its stability depends on active control. For the first tests on a biped you need a support or a harness and clear space around it.
  • A written preliminary risk assessment, shared with the company's health and safety officer.
  • Operators trained on the emergency stop and on recovery procedures.

Further reading: Unitree robots and the Machinery Regulation 2027.

Buy, rent or simulate

A PoC does not always mean buying the robot straight away. In some cases a first check is done in simulation; in others you can consider renting or a short-term pilot, as explained on the Assessment and PoC page. If buying is the chosen route, it is worth checking first whether the project can use UK reliefs such as capital allowances, R&D tax relief or regional adoption programmes for manufacturers: requirements and official sources are on the Financing page. We can provide the quote and technical documentation for your claim but do not give tax advice: the eligibility of the specific asset should be confirmed by your accountant.

What the final report must contain

  1. KPIs measured against the plan's thresholds, with the number of trials.
  2. Causes of failures, broken down by category (perception, grasping, navigation, software, hardware).
  3. Estimated total cost to move to the next phase: hardware, software, safety, training.
  4. Recommendation: go, no-go or revision of the scope.

A PoC that does not reach its KPIs is not wasted time: it is information that avoids a wrong order. If the numbers do not hold up, it is better to know before buying ten units.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a PoC with a humanoid robot take?

It depends on the case. Mapping, comparing solutions, simulation and the PoC plan take roughly 5–9 weeks for a single use case; execution and decision are fixed in the plan. If a new robot is needed, delivery of humanoids is indicated at 4–6 weeks.

Which Unitree humanoid is best for a pick-and-place PoC?

For picking and placing on a bench, the G1-D Standard on a fixed stand (from €38,005 excluding VAT) avoids the risk of falling. On a tight budget you can consider the R1-D, priced on request.

Which KPIs should I measure in a humanoid PoC?

Success rate, cycle time, human interventions per hour, real battery life per charge and setup time. They must be written in the plan with a threshold value before testing starts.

Do I have to buy the robot to run a PoC?

Not always. In some cases the first check is done in simulation, in others you can consider renting or a short-term pilot. The choice depends on the case and on the maturity of the process.

Which safety rules apply to a humanoid PoC?

Today the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC; from 20 January 2027 Regulation (EU) 2023/1230. For industrial robots the reference is the ISO 10218 series. A risk assessment shared with the health and safety officer is required.
